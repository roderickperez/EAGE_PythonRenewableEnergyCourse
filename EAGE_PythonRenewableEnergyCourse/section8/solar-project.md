# Project 2: Solar Energy

Design a small solar farm and investigate how location, season, panel orientation and weather affect its output. The explorer combines a location-based solar path with an illustrative weather scenario and simplified PV model. It is a learning activity, not a site assessment or forecast.

## How to use the explorer

1. Select a highlighted location on the spinning globe or choose one of the five regional cards to move directly to the farm grid. The globe uses public-domain [Natural Earth land data](https://www.naturalearthdata.com/); the selected site's coordinates determine the calculations.
2. Start with **\$5,000**. Click a grid cell to buy a panel for **\$1,000**; click it again to remove it and recover **\$500**. Each panel represents 2 m² at 20% efficiency, or 400 W at standard test conditions. Drag the farm to rotate the view and scroll to zoom. To use the keyboard, focus the scene, move the gold selection with the arrow keys and press Enter or Space.
3. Adjust panel tilt and azimuth. Azimuth is clockwise from north: north 0°, east 90°, south 180° and west 270°. All panels share the same settings. Select the calendar to set the date and adjust solar time or allow the simulation to advance. Use the speed slider to accelerate the clock, clouds, energy and revenue together. The sun and dashed daylight path correspond to the chosen latitude and date; the clock shows solar time. Use the square button in the upper-right corner of the scene to pause or resume, or the globe button in the lower-left corner to change location.
4. Compare current AC power and the **Power over the solar day** graph. Each colored curve shows a complete simulated day for a numbered panel, including clouds moving over it. The gold line marks the current solar time. The curves use the current cloud paths, panel settings and weather throughout the day, so later random weather changes can alter them. The day's estimated energy integrates these curves; cumulative energy records the evolving simulation.

## Time, weather and budget

At 1× speed, the target is one solar day in 60 seconds of visible time; delayed animation frames can make actual playback slower. The default is **20×**; the speed slider ranges from 1× to 500×, with intermediate simulation steps used to calculate generation and revenue. The calendar advances at midnight, and the location's latitude and day of year set solar declination and the sun's path. Time is **local solar time**: noon is when the sun crosses the local meridian. Longitude identifies the selected site but does not change solar-time sun angles; no time-zone or equation-of-time correction is applied. The scene darkens when the sun is below the horizon. Pause the simulation to compare configurations under the same conditions. Changing location restarts the project and its budget.

Cloud cover and air temperature follow a simple latitude-dependent seasonal pattern; cloud cover also varies randomly through the day, and panel surface losses vary slightly from day to day. Clouds travel across the scene in a constant direction. Their shadows are projected onto the site along the sun's direction. To avoid near-horizontal shadow rays, the projected cloud-shadow effect is disabled at solar elevations of approximately 1.15° or less, even though the simplified irradiance model can produce power above the horizon. Shadows visibly sweep over the panel surfaces, while each panel's power samples the cloud loss at its centre; the simplified electrical model does not calculate partial-shading mismatch. Farm power sums the individual panel outputs. The full-day curves and estimated energy use moving clouds and the current weather scenario; random weather changes later in the day are not predicted, so these curves are illustrative rather than a record of the day's actual generation. These conditions are synthetic, not historical measurements or a location-specific climate model. The game credits **\$0.10 per simulated kWh**; these fixed costs, salvage and revenue are teaching values, not actual project economics. Energy and revenue stop when paused or the page is hidden. Changing date, time or settings does not retroactively change accumulated energy.

## The model

The explorer uses the solar lesson's sun-position and plane-of-array calculations. It resolves direct, isotropic diffuse sky, and ground-reflected irradiance on the tilted panel. A simplified irradiance profile responds to synthetic cloud cover. Panel output includes a cell-temperature correction, shading/soiling, wiring loss and inverter efficiency, with the inverter limited to 400 W per panel. Panels do not shade one another; the spacing and panel dimensions in the 3D scene are illustrative.

The referenced articles discuss using open-source data and panel tilt, orientation, shading and irradiance to estimate generation, and using `pvlib` for solar calculations: [Estimating Solar Panel Output with Open-Source Data](https://towardsdatascience.com/estimating-solar-panel-output-with-open-source-data-bbca6ea1f523/) and [Solar panel power generation analysis](https://towardsdatascience.com/solar-panel-power-generation-analysis-7011cc078900/). Read those articles critically: diffuse horizontal irradiance is sky radiation scattered by the atmosphere, excluding the direct beam; an oblique direct beam is still direct radiation ([Sandia definition](https://pvpmc.sandia.gov/modeling-guide/1-weather-design-inputs/irradiance-insolation/diffuse-horizontal-irradiance/)). South-facing panels are a useful UK starting point, not a universal optimum: orientation and tilt depend on location and the objective. One article also uses inconsistent azimuth signs; PVGIS aspect is zero south and positive west, while the explorer uses azimuth clockwise from north. Convert a PVGIS aspect to this convention as `(aspect + 180) % 360`; check the convention of the particular interface before transferring angles ([PVGIS API](https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/photovoltaic-geographical-information-system-pvgis/using-pvgis-5/api-non-interactive-service_en)).

The second article's [linked notebook](https://github.com/lmeulen/SolarPower/blob/main/SolarPower.ipynb) uses an older PVGIS interface and needs review before reuse. In pvlib, `surface_azimuth` changed to the north-zero convention in version 0.10.0, and `get_pvgis_hourly` changed from three return values to two `(data, metadata)` in version 0.13.0. Check the installed version, convert the angle for that interface, and update any return-value unpacking; verify cardinal directions with a small test. See the [pvlib function documentation](https://pvlib-python.readthedocs.io/en/stable/reference/generated/pvlib.iotools.get_pvgis_hourly.html).

This browser simulation uses the course's simplified JavaScript model; it does not fetch measured irradiance, perform rooftop mapping, or run `pvlib`. Its results require measured weather, equipment and site data for real-world validation.
